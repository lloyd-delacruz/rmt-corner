Headaches that come from your neck — and what helps.

Cervicogenic headaches — what they are, why they're so often misdiagnosed, and what treatment looks like.

Kyrill Fourth, RMT January 25, 2026 7 min read

Roughly one in five chronic headaches is cervicogenic — meaning it originates in the neck and refers up into the head — and roughly half of those are misdiagnosed as either tension headaches or migraines. The treatment for the three is different. So is the prognosis. So getting the call right matters.

This is the version of the explanation we'd give a friend at dinner.

What a cervicogenic headache actually is

The top three vertebrae of your neck — C1, C2, and C3 — share a nerve supply with parts of the face and head, particularly the back of the skull, the temples, the orbit around the eye, and (sometimes) the jaw. When the joints, muscles, or fascia around those vertebrae are restricted, irritated, or chronically tight, the brain can interpret the signal as a headache rather than a neck issue.

The pain is real. It's just coming from a different address than where it's being felt.

How it's usually misdiagnosed

It looks like a tension headache because it sits across the back and sides of the head. It can look like a migraine because it can include light sensitivity, mild nausea, and eye pain. It does not respond well to standard headache medication, which is the clue that often gets missed.

If you have:

  • Headaches that start in the back of the neck or base of the skull and travel up
  • Headaches that are worse after long bouts of sitting or screen work
  • One-sided headaches that don't switch sides between episodes
  • Headaches that you can provoke by pressing certain spots in your neck or upper back
  • A limited range of neck rotation on the same side as the headache

...the cervicogenic flag is up. Worth investigating.

The mechanism, briefly

Most cervicogenic headache patterns we see in clinic come from a small group of suspects: chronic forward head posture (the screen-and-phone position), an old whiplash that was never properly rehabilitated, sustained jaw clenching, and overuse of the suboccipital muscles — the small group at the very top of the neck that holds your skull on. Of these, the suboccipitals are the most common single cause.

You can find them by tilting your head back and feeling along the line where your skull meets your neck. The tender knot most people find about an inch below the base of the skull, slightly off-centre on each side, is the upper trap and suboccipital cluster. When that group is locked tight, it pulls on the dura — the connective tissue lining the skull — and pulls referred pain up over the head.

The pain is real. The address is just wrong.

What treatment looks like

For an established cervicogenic pattern, we typically see clients weekly for the first three to four weeks. The work is precise rather than heavy — the suboccipital region is small, layered, and adjacent to important structures (the vertebral artery, the upper cervical nerve roots), so the depth and pace matter more than the pressure.

A typical session looks like:

  • Ten minutes of warmup work along the upper traps, levator scapulae, and posterior neck
  • Twenty minutes of focused work on the suboccipital region — often including held positional release, where we hold a specific pressure for ninety seconds rather than working through it
  • Ten minutes of jaw and temporal work, since the systems are linked
  • Ten minutes of thoracic work, because the upper back is almost always part of the picture

Most clients notice a reduction in headache frequency by the third or fourth visit. Most achieve significant improvement within eight to twelve weeks. After that, we taper to monthly maintenance and home-care.

What you can do at home

Three things help between sessions:

  1. Reduce screen-and-phone time, particularly with the head dropped. Every degree of forward head position increases load on the upper neck non-linearly. Phones held at chest height are the worst single posture; raising the phone to eye level is the simplest reduction.
  2. Train the deep neck flexors, the small group that should be holding your head over your shoulders rather than out in front. The chin-tuck exercise from our desk reset piece is the right starting point — ten reps, three times a day.
  3. Heat on the suboccipital region for ten minutes before bed. A small heating pad or a warm wet towel. This won't fix anything by itself but it shortens the recovery window between sessions.

When to see someone other than us

A few patterns are not for massage therapy and should be checked by a physician first:

  • A new headache pattern after age fifty
  • A headache with neurological symptoms (visual changes, weakness, speech changes, numbness)
  • A headache after a head injury
  • A headache with fever, stiff neck, or rash
  • A headache that wakes you from sleep, particularly if it's worsening over weeks

None of these are cervicogenic by themselves. All of them deserve a doctor's review before you come to us.

For everyone else: if your headaches start in your neck and your usual painkillers aren't helping, this is a treatable pattern. Book a session and we'll work through it.
