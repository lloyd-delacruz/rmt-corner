Recovery

What to do — and not do — the day after deep tissue.

Hydration, light movement, and a quick reality check on the eight-glasses myth.

Mark Todd, RMT February 8, 2026 5 min read

The day after a deep tissue session is when most people accidentally undo the work. Not because they do anything dramatic, but because the internet has produced a lot of confident bad advice about post-treatment care, and it's easy to follow. Here is the short, practical version. We will skip the wellness vocabulary.

Hydration — the actual story

You don't need to drink more water than usual after a massage. The "flush out the toxins" line — beloved of spa receptionists — is not a thing. Lymphatic and circulatory systems aren't reservoirs that you flood with water; they're moving constantly, and your kidneys clear them as a matter of routine.

What is true: deep tissue work can leave you mildly dehydrated if your baseline was already low (long flights, lots of caffeine, an active morning before the session). In that case, drink to thirst. A glass of water when you leave, water with dinner. That's the whole protocol. The eight-glasses-a-day rule was made up in 1945 and has been thoroughly debunked since.

Move, don't rest

The temptation after a session is to come home and not move for the rest of the night. Don't. A short walk after dinner — twenty minutes is plenty — does more for next-day soreness than any other single thing. Tissue that has been worked then immobilised stiffens overnight. Tissue that has been worked and gently used over the next eight hours does not.

This isn't a workout. It's a circulation walk. Your heart rate barely changes. The point is to keep blood and lymph moving through the area we treated.

Heat, not ice

For the soreness most people get the day after a deep tissue session, heat is the right answer. A warm bath, a heating pad on the sore area, a hot shower. Ice has uses — for acute injury within the first twenty-four hours, for sharp inflammatory pain — but for the diffuse, dull, post-treatment ache, heat helps more.

If you have a bath, fifteen minutes is enough. Don't fall asleep in it.

Skip the foam roller

The post-treatment foam roller is one of those things people do to feel proactive. Mostly it makes the sore tissue more sore. The work has already been done by the therapist; the tissue is in a recovery window. Foam-rolling the same area on the same day is the equivalent of going to the gym after a hard workout because you didn't quite feel done.

Save the foam roller for two days later, on a different area, at low pressure.

Sleep the extra hour

If you can find a way to sleep an extra hour the night of treatment, do. The body does most of its tissue repair during sleep, and a deep tissue session creates a small repair load. An extra hour of sleep that night is worth more than any supplement on the market for it.

Walk a little. Heat. Sleep more than usual. Skip the foam roller. That's the list.

What doesn't help (despite advertising)

  • Magnesium oil sprays — the skin doesn't absorb meaningful magnesium; this is a confidence trick repeated often enough to feel true.
  • Compression sleeves — useful for circulation in flights or post-surgical contexts. Not useful for post-massage soreness.
  • Cupping the same day — too much input on already-irritated tissue. Wait at least 48 hours.
  • Aggressive stretching — light range-of-motion is fine, but pulling deep into a stretch on tissue that's just been worked is how soreness becomes strain.
  • Anti-inflammatory medication "to be safe" — if you have pain, take what you need. If you don't, don't. NSAIDs taken prophylactically slow tissue repair; this is well-documented in sports medicine literature.

If something feels off

Most post-session soreness is normal and fades inside forty-eight hours. The exceptions are covered in our piece on when soreness isn't normal. If you're not sure, ring the clinic. The phones are answered during the same hours we treat.

— Mark
