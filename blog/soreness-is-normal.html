Why some soreness is normal — and how to tell when it isn't.

Post-treatment soreness, DOMS, and the line between useful discomfort and a reason to ring us.

Mark Todd, RMT April 2, 2026 5 min read

Soreness after massage sits in an awkward place. It's common enough that most therapists don't bother warning you about it — but uncommon enough that, the first time it happens, plenty of clients quietly assume something went wrong. We get the email a couple of times a month. Almost always it's nothing. Sometimes it isn't. Here is how we think about the difference.

The short answer

Some soreness after deep work is expected. Most of it should be mild, fade within forty-eight hours, and feel more like a dull ache than a sharp pain. Anything outside those three lines deserves a phone call.

What's actually happening

When we work through tissue that's been holding tension for weeks or months, three things change at once. Local circulation increases. Adhesions between muscle layers loosen. The nervous system steps off the gas. Each of those is a useful thing. Each of them can also produce a low-grade inflammatory response — the same response you'd get from a moderately hard workout — and that's the soreness you might feel the next day.

It's a cousin of DOMS (delayed-onset muscle soreness). Same family of mechanism, gentler dose. It tends to peak around twenty-four hours after the session and resolve by forty-eight.

The patterns that are normal

  • A dull ache in the muscle group we worked. Tender to press, fine to move.
  • Mild stiffness first thing in the morning that loosens within ten or fifteen minutes of moving around.
  • Referred soreness in nearby tissue — the day after working an upper trap, the same-side jaw or lower neck might feel tender. The body releases as a chain.
  • Brief tiredness or sleepiness for the rest of the session day. Deep tissue shifts the nervous system, and the system pays for the shift.

Mild, dull, and fading. Those are the three words.

The patterns that aren't

Get in touch if any of these turn up:

  • Sharp or nerve-like pain — stabbing, electric, zinging, or accompanied by numbness or tingling down a limb.
  • Bruising larger than a coin, or anywhere we didn't work directly.
  • Soreness that's getting worse on day three, rather than fading.
  • A new headache with nausea, dizziness, or visual changes — particularly after upper-cervical work.
  • Reduced range of motion rather than improved.
  • A flare in a pre-existing condition we discussed at intake.

None of these are common. None of them are emergencies in most cases. All of them are useful information for us, and easier to address sooner than later.

What helps in the first twenty-four hours

The shortest list we can give you:

If you're sore

Walk for fifteen minutes. Take a warm bath or shower. Sleep an extra hour. Eat protein. Skip the gym, the foam roller, and any aggressive stretching of the area we worked.

That's most of what you can do. Tissue repair runs on time and rest. Adding things rarely speeds it up; subtracting the wrong things — like sleep, or food — slows it down.

One thing we'd ask

If your last session left you sore for longer than two days, or rougher than you'd expected, please tell us at the next visit. We adjust depth, pace, and focus per person, and we can only calibrate if we know how your tissue responded. The therapist who treated you wants to know. They are not offended by the feedback. They are offended only by silence followed by you not coming back.

For the longer version of what to do in the day or two after treatment, we wrote a separate piece on the day after deep tissue.

— Mark
