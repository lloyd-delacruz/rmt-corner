Posture

A two-minute desk reset that actually works.

A short sequence for desk-bound bodies — anchored in joint mechanics rather than wellness vibes.

Kylie Tarry, RMT March 22, 2026 4 min read

Most office stretching advice is bad. Not wrong, exactly — just decorative. The neck rolls and the wrist shakes don't address the joints that actually take the load when you sit at a screen for eight hours. They feel like something. They don't do much.

Below is what we give to clients who ask for a desk-side sequence that earns the time it costs. It takes about two minutes, requires no equipment, and targets the five areas that give desk workers the most trouble: thoracic spine, hip flexors, anterior shoulders, deep neck flexors, and ankles.

The sequence

1 · Thoracic extension over the chair back · 30 seconds

Sit forward on your chair. Lace fingers behind your head, elbows wide. Lean back over the top of the chair until you feel a stretch through the upper back. Breathe in slowly through the nose. Don't force it — the goal is a controlled arch, not maximum range. Your upper back is the joint that loses mobility first when you sit. Give it back.

2 · Hip flexor lunge against the desk · 20 seconds per side

Step one foot back into a deep lunge. Tuck the back hip under (think: pelvis drops down, not forward). Hold the desk for balance. You should feel this in the front of the back hip and thigh. Sitting shortens these tissues by about three inches over the course of a working day; this is the antidote.

3 · Doorway pec stretch · 20 seconds per side

Forearm against a doorframe, elbow at shoulder height. Step the same-side foot forward, rotate your chest gently away. The stretch should land in the front of the chest and shoulder. Don't push past mild — this is a tissue that bruises easily if you're aggressive.

4 · Chin tucks against the wall · 10 reps

Stand against a wall, heels six inches off it. Tuck your chin straight back (give yourself a double chin) without tilting your head down. Hold for two seconds, release. Ten reps. This re-engages the deep neck flexors that hold your head over your shoulders rather than out in front of them.

5 · Calf raises with pause · 12 reps

Two feet, hip-width. Rise onto the balls of your feet, hold three seconds at the top, lower with control. Twelve reps. The calves are the one muscle group that runs your circulation back up the legs while you sit; loading them briefly resets the venous return.

The point isn't flexibility. It's reversing what eight hours of sitting did.

When to do it

Mid-afternoon is the most useful window — long enough into the day that you've accumulated load, far enough from the end that the rest of the afternoon benefits. If you can manage twice a day (mid-morning and mid-afternoon), that's better still.

You don't need a mat. You don't need to leave your desk. You don't need to look like you're doing yoga at work. The whole sequence is unobtrusive enough to do in office wear.

What it won't fix

This is maintenance, not therapy. If you're already in pain, two minutes of stretching won't reach a tissue that's been compressed for years. That's the kind of pattern that's better unwound on the table — by hand, with assessment, slowly. We see this in clinic three times a week. Stretching is the after-care, not the treatment.

If your neck or shoulders are giving you ongoing trouble, see our piece on neck-related headaches or book a session and we'll work it out together.

— Kylie
