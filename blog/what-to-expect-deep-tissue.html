What to expect after your first deep tissue session.

A plain-language guide to the 48 hours after your first deep tissue massage — what's normal, what isn't, and the home-care that actually helps.

Mark Todd, RMT April 14, 2026 8 min read

You'll walk out feeling looser than you came in. Maybe a little floppy. By dinner, that lightness might have shifted — into a heavy, low-grade ache, the kind that makes you want to sit down rather than stretch. By tomorrow, parts of you you didn't think were sore will be.

This is normal. It's also not the only outcome. Some people leave a deep tissue session and feel nothing but better, all the way through. Others have thirty-six hours of mild soreness. A small number have a rougher forty-eight — usually the ones whose tissue had a lot to give up.

What follows is what we tell first-timers on their way out the door. None of it is a substitute for ringing us if something feels wrong. Most of it will sound obvious. The point of writing it down is so you don't have to remember at six o'clock when your shoulders start to talk to you.

The first six hours

In the immediate window, expect:

  • A noticeable drop in baseline tension. You'll move with less effort. Stairs feel different.
  • Mild heat or tingling in the areas we worked. Local circulation is responding. It's not damage.
  • Sleepiness. Deep tissue work shifts the nervous system toward parasympathetic — the rest-and-digest side. Plan a quiet evening.
  • Thirst. Drink to thirst, not to a quota. The eight-glasses rule is a myth (we wrote about that here).

Avoid heavy training, alcohol, and big inflammatory meals for the rest of the day. None of these will undo the session. They'll just make tomorrow harder than it needs to be.

Twelve to forty-eight hours

This is where most of the soreness lives. Two patterns are normal:

  1. A targeted ache in the muscle group we worked. Feels like the day after a hard but reasonable workout. Tender to press, fine to move.
  2. Referred soreness somewhere we didn't touch. This sounds odd but it's common. Working a tight upper trap can leave the same-side jaw or temple sore the next day, because the system you released was holding compensations elsewhere.

Both fade. The targeted ache resolves in twenty-four to thirty-six hours. Referred soreness usually goes within forty-eight.

The body is a chain. When we change one link, the others reorganize.

What's worth a phone call

The line between useful soreness and something that needs attention isn't fuzzy. Get in touch if you notice any of these in the first seventy-two hours:

  • Sharp pain, not ache. Pain that's stabbing or feels nerve-like — zinging, electric, numbing.
  • Bruising larger than a coin, or that appeared without an obvious cause.
  • Headache with nausea, dizziness, or visual changes — particularly after upper-cervical work.
  • Soreness that's getting worse on day three rather than fading.
  • A flare in a pre-existing condition we discussed at intake.

None of these are common. All of them deserve a quick check-in. Email or call — we'd much rather hear from you and tell you it's fine than have you wait it out.

Home-care that actually helps

The internet will tell you to do a lot of things. Most of them don't matter much. These do.

Move gently, often

A short walk is better than ninety minutes of stillness. Five-minute movement breaks every hour or two keep tissue from re-tightening overnight.

Heat over ice

For post-session soreness specifically, warmth helps. A bath, a heating pad, a warm shower. Ice has a place — but rarely the day after deep tissue.

Sleep

This is the one that's easy to skip and the one that does the most. Tissue repair runs on sleep. Aim for an extra hour if you can find it.

Skip the aggressive stretching

Light range-of-motion is fine. Pulling deep into a stretch on already-irritated tissue is how soreness becomes strain.

Eat a real dinner

Protein, vegetables, something warm. The body needs the building blocks.

In short

Walk a little. Heat, not ice. Sleep more than usual. Eat real food. Don't yank yourself into a stretch. That's the whole list.

When to book the next one

For most first-timers, the second session is more useful when it lands ten to fourteen days after the first — long enough for the work to settle, soon enough that the tissue hasn't reverted.

If you came in for a specific issue — low-back pain, frozen shoulder, post-injury work — we'll usually map a longer plan together at the second visit, once we've seen how your tissue responds to the first.

If you came in for general maintenance, every four to six weeks is a reasonable cadence for most desks-and-screens lives. Some people benefit from more, some from less. There's a longer piece on this in how often you should book a massage.

One last thing

A first session is also a calibration. If something we did was too much, or not enough, tell us at the next visit. Therapists adjust depth, pace, and focus per person — but only if we know. Quiet patient politeness is the enemy of a good treatment plan.

Most people leave their first deep tissue session and come back. That's the point of doing it well. Soreness for a day, looser for a month — usually a fair trade.

Welcome to the practice.

— Mark
